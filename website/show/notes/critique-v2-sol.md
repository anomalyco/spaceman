## Verdict

**F is the film. D has the best material. E is an explainer wearing a witness’s suit.**

The central danger: making an ordinary controlling man into someone who deliberately writes amusingly sinister window titles. His documents must serve purposes. The audience supplies the diagnosis.

## Fix these factual problems first

- **⌥1 switches workspaces; it is not a “tile everything” command.** Let the visitor’s input start the staged animation and activate workspace 1. Don’t teach a false shortcut.
- **Your exceptions are shadowed.** In all three drafts, broad `Chatter → 2` precedes the Dana exception. First match wins. Put exact-title exceptions above app-wide rules.
- E’s `Daybook, title "Talk"` does **not** match `Talk — everyone (no agenda)`. Match the entire window title.
- E’s existing Daybook routing also conflicts with the invite arriving on 1. Don’t assume saving a rule retrospectively moves an existing window. For that existing invite, demonstrate **⌥⇧0**.
- **Float does not mean always-on-top.** “Above the layout” promises something different.
- These arrows are explanatory notation, not established “real Spaceman syntax.”
- F’s download line introduces an unestablished multiple-display claim. Remove it.
- Optional visitor switching cannot carry essential exposition. Script one clear switch and return; enlarge the bar briefly before 0:40.

## D — “A place for everything”

### What works

- **“I kept the real one.”** Credible decision, absurdly consequential delivery.
- **“He said I didn’t need to.”** Excellent. Someone offered forgiveness; he completed the task instead.
- **“Nobody wanted to take the minutes. So I take the minutes.”** The whole character.
- The hedge measurements. Specificity makes the obsession both credible and funny.
- **“The committee is more efficient now. There’s less disagreement.”** Good, provided the image establishes what happened to membership.
- Equal family tiles: a clean visual expression of his mistake.

### What fails

- **“I visit.”** A polished little button. He can be serious, but this sounds written for applause.
- **“It’s not gone. It’s on two.”** Functional clarification, not a laugh. Use once.
- **`Bins — Collection Compliance`** needs an actual inspection or reporting function. Otherwise it is a comedy writer replacing “bins” with bureaucracy.
- **`Committee — Membership (1)`** is ambiguous: one member or a duplicate document? Show a member count inside the window.
- **“You have a lot of windows open.” / “I can help with that.”** Stock menacing-helper dialogue. Suddenly he knows he is in a trailer.
- **“Please use them for windows.”** Explicit wink. The copywriter comes out to explain the transgression.

The mundane titles generally pass, including `Dinner (Fri) — Agenda`: that is exactly the document this man would make.

### Build, ending, delivery

The dread genuinely develops: self-management → household management → jurisdiction over others. Best escalation of the three.

The bezel crossing throws that away for a familiar “the website is alive” stunt. It also makes the product appear capable of manipulating a webpage’s contents.

At an unhurried delivery, roughly 200 words plus holds put this close to or beyond the limit. The 1:35 ending needs more room than it has. A fast reader gets hedge surveillance and committee attrition; a listener gets the character but misses Dana and the unreadable late workspaces.

## E — “Procedure”

### What works

- **“Three of the windows were the same note. I closed two.”** Excellent accounting.
- **“Mail was assigned to workspace two.”** The passive voice is character, not decoration.
- **“That distinction became important.”** A usable promise—but the ending must actually make that distinction matter.
- **“I found that very calming.”** Credible admission after the first-match explanation.
- **“Rule thirty-four.”** Potentially strong if the preceding action is morally disproportionate.

### What fails

- **“My mother floats.”** Pun.
- **“I think she’d like that.”** Explains the pun.
- **“Dana is ignored.”** Sets up another pun.
- **“She can stay wherever she likes.”** Supplies its reassuring punchline. This entire passage knows it is funny.
- **`Talk — everyone (no agenda)`** is engineered to advertise his inability to tolerate spontaneity. A real invite would more likely be `Sunday`.
- **“Everything is where it should be.”** Predictable final thesis.
- **“Rules match apps and window titles.”** Documentation, not an ending.

The remaining functional lines pass as sincere speech. Most are exposition rather than comedy.

### Build, ending, delivery

The numbered rules accumulate, but the dramatic situation barely changes. Faster scrolling and dimmer lighting cannot substitute for consequence.

The final action is sad avoidance, not a surprise. We already know he routes inconvenient things elsewhere. Routing one more thing elsewhere confirms the premise.

This is also the most cognitively expensive first half: narration, rule ordering, title matching, bar, keyboard commands. A fast reader catches the contradictions; a listener hears a long tutorial. The four-second invite hold is good, but it needs a shorter film around it.

## F — “Ten”

### What works

- **“It took about a second.”** Properly modest against a disproportionate life change.
- **“The house has a workspace.”** Serious, economical, faintly wrong.
- **“Everyone gets the same amount of space.”** Best retained here, without further commentary.
- Returning to changed workspaces makes time visible rather than narrated.
- The foreign keypress is excellent sound dramaturgy.

### What fails

- **`Groceries — Standing Order (Revised, Final)`** is a familiar office-comedy title. Show quantities changing instead.
- **`Contingency (Household)`** signals “ominous document” without communicating anything.
- **“I wasn’t sure what to put here.”** Transparently positions the empty space for the reveal. Silence is stronger.
- Ten chapter numbers are not ten developed chapters. Workspaces 7–9 currently function as trailer inserts.

### Build, ending, delivery

Strongest ending: it changes who has been doing the observing. But nested-screen reveals are familiar. Earn this one with a tiny earlier sign of outside control—a cursor movement he does not initiate, which he patiently waits out.

It fits 100 seconds. The danger is inertness, not overcrowding. Ten seconds of hedge photos needs visible progression, not ten seconds spent understanding one title.

Fast readers get the story; listeners currently get very little. Keep three or four of D’s character lines. The first-half demo still needs explicit labels for moving versus switching.

## Two alternative endings

### 1. Remote support

Earlier, establish No. 14’s printer problem in an ordinary message. At the end, a remote-session toolbar appears: **`Connected to: No. 14`**. The supposedly personal desktop belongs to the neighbour whose hedge he has been documenting.

His cursor opens another household document.

**“The printer was working by Tuesday.”**

Hard cut. Download card: **For your windows.**

The joke is the monstrous expansion of a small, legitimate assignment.

### 2. Quorum

The four family Call tiles appear normal. One participant adjusts his camera. All four adjust simultaneously.

They are four sessions belonging to him, each labelled with a different committee role. In the minutes, he records **`Quorum confirmed`**. No musical sting.

**“We were able to proceed.”**

Hard cut. Download card: **For your windows.**

Seed the committee roles earlier. Otherwise this is merely a surreal reveal.
