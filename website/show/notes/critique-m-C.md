## 1. Verdict

The tape left as a housewarming gift is a genuinely nasty idea, but this draft lands as a threatening neighbor’s introduction, not yet as a devastating revelation. It announces the menace three times, then asks a complicated final camera move to explain who is listening and why.

## 2. Does the reveal land?

**The threat lands. The recording’s identity does not land cleanly enough.**

There are three separate discoveries available here:

1. This apparently helpful man has made monitoring his neighbors into an administrative system.
2. Something happened to the previous occupant of No. 14, and he may be responsible.
3. This recording was left for the next occupant as a warning disguised as a welcome.

The first works. The second is insinuated but unproved. The third is the actual twist—and it currently lives partly in your introductory explanation, which the audience will not have.

“The man who had your house” reveals the intended listener quite early. “He doesn’t live there now” signals the dark turn. “I’d keep it that way” makes the threat explicit. By “Welcome to number fourteen,” there is little left to discover verbally.

Then “Your light’s on” attempts another reveal: **he can see you now.** But this is a recording made years earlier. A prerecorded threat can certainly anticipate its listener, but the staging makes it feel like a live observation without earning that effect. It risks supernatural omniscience—or simply a writer’s closing sting.

### What a first-time viewer understands at the cut

Probably:

> This obsessive neighbor intimidated, expelled, or possibly killed the previous owner over a hedge. I’m now the new owner, and he is still across the street.

They will **not necessarily understand**:

- That the recording is years old.
- That it was physically left in the house.
- Whether the final desktop belongs to the new owner or is another workspace on the original machine.
- Whether the reversed doorbell view means continuing surveillance, reciprocal surveillance, or a change of narrator.

The ending is therefore **too explicit about the threat and too obscure about the physical situation**.

The best material is not the threatening dialogue. It is the minutes nobody attends, the repeated circulation, and the board call continuing after the other person leaves. That is where the film becomes disturbing without putting on a disturbing voice. The script should stop interpreting those images for us.

## 3. The five weakest lines

### 1. “Mail went to two. The internet went to three. It isn’t closed. It’s on two.”

**Problem:** “It” appears to refer to the browser, which you have just placed on three. The wording also claims persistence without giving the return visit any dramatic weight.

**Rewrite:**

> “I moved Mail to two. The browser to three. I went back to check. They were both still there.”

Let the actual switching establish what “went back” means.

### 2. “I wrote a rule for my friend’s messages. They open on two.”

**Problem:** This sounds like sender-based message routing. Your product routes applications or window titles, not individual friends’ messages.

**Rewrite:**

> “I made a rule. Chatter opens on two.”

Then Dana’s message disappears with Chatter. His treatment of the message supplies the emotional meaning; the narration should not explain it.

### 3. “After that I photographed it every morning at six-oh-two.”

**Problem:** Six-foot-two photographed at six-oh-two is the writer arranging a little joke. It makes him a designed eccentric rather than a person whose reasonable habits have become monstrous.

**Rewrite:**

> “After that I photographed it before breakfast. Same place each morning.”

Keep “A hundred and sixty mornings.” The count is good. The numerical rhyme is not.

### 4. “The hedge is six feet. I’d keep it that way.”

**Problem:** The second sentence converts administrative certainty into a stock screen threat. He briefly becomes a villain delivering his warning.

**Rewrite:**

> “It’s six feet now.”

That gives the measurement the status of a completed task. Much colder. Let the missing occupant sit beside that fact.

### 5. “Your light’s on.”

**Problem:** It sacrifices the recording’s credibility for a familiar horror button. It also makes the earlier light observation feel planted exclusively to service this line.

**Rewrite:**

> “I thought you should have a copy.”

This makes the tape an intentionally delivered record, not an accidentally discovered confession or an inexplicably live transmission. It is ordinary language carrying an appalling purpose.

Delivered after “Welcome to number fourteen,” it also preserves his belief that he has done something considerate.

## 4. Rule breaks and execution risks

### Product truth

- **Automatic tiling needs clearer causality.** “Then everything went side by side” comes after closing the notes, making that action sound like the trigger. Your stated film tiles first, then closes duplicates. Align the narration with the picture.
- **The message rule is currently misleading.** Fix as above.
- **The keyboard commands are absent.** They need not be spoken, but they must be unmistakable on screen:
  - **Option+Shift+2/3:** move the focused window.
  - **Option+2/3:** switch workspaces.
  
  Don’t show one shortcut while the other action happens.
- **Prove the distinction between moving and switching.** The first half should show him move Mail, remain where he is, then switch to find it. Otherwise viewers may understand only “numbers make applications disappear.”
- **“Four is the house. Five is the street” is fine as his private shorthand.** Do not turn those into named workspace labels.
- **The bottom bar is described correctly**, but its contents need to remain readable during the demonstration. A decorative strip will not substantiate “where everything is.”

### Tone

The principal winks are **six-foot-two/six-oh-two**, **“I’d keep it that way,”** and **“Your light’s on.”** Remove those flourishes and the ordinary material gets considerably more frightening.

Keep the drill. “He said I didn’t need to” sounds like a real person remembering an interaction he has chosen to misunderstand. Do not underline it with a sinister drill close-up.

### Harm / real people

No obvious violation. The wrongdoing is implied, and there are no actionable instructions for harm. Keep the people, addresses, correspondence, and HOA documents unmistakably fictional.

### Timing and recording

At roughly **205 words**, this can fit the requested duration, but a 100-second film leaves limited room for the interaction, pauses, months passing, and the final geographical clarification.

Record the visitor’s six seconds of space into the continuous performance. Don’t solve the schedule by rushing the actor or suddenly stopping the tape playback.

## 5. What the final picture should show

**The final picture must prove that the recording was left for this listener. It does not need another surveillance gimmick.**

Keep the arrival at No. 14, but remove the reversed doorbell feed. Watching No. 12 through another camera introduces a second surveillance arrangement when you should be resolving the first.

The final tableau:

- An ordinary desktop belonging to the new occupant of **No. 14**—not another ominous collection of hedge photographs.
- A simple audio player, clearly playing a transfer of a cassette labeled **“No. 14,” dated several years earlier**. Establish the old date legibly, not as forensic metadata.
- Beyond the screen, through the actual window, **No. 12’s study is still lit**. Its window arrangement unmistakably matches the room the camera just left.
- No figure stepping into view. No fresh notification. No additional threatening sentence.
- Hold long enough to understand the relationship. Then hard cut.

Lose **“found in kitchen drawer”** as a window title. That is a production note masquerading as interface text.

The visual discovery should be:

> **This was not evidence someone found after the fact. He meant the next owner to hear it. And he is still there.**

That is the strongest reveal this premise currently supports. If you want the audience to know he caused the previous owner’s disappearance, the present draft does not establish it—and making him sound more threatening will not supply the missing evidence.
