## Verdict

**A is the best foundation. C has the strongest visual ending. B has the best individual straight-man joke.** None yet earns its final escalation: shouting replaces comic invention precisely when the pictures should become funniest.

Two shared fixes:

- **Fix the start contract.** All three spend 15–17 seconds asking for the key that supposedly starts the show. Make the messy desktop the waiting state; start the timed show on ⌥1/tap. Don’t auto-start.
- **Separate real behavior from villain fantasy.** Establish app-based, ordered first-match rules. Routing Chatter doesn’t silence notifications; “ignore” doesn’t banish an app. Named A–Z workspaces require opt-in, not spontaneous alphabet growth. Show ⌥0 for workspace 10 somewhere.

## A — “Gentle productivity”

### What works
- The sticky getting buried is a complete visual joke.
- “The internet is on three, where it can’t hurt me” gives an ordinary feature emotional meaning.
- “Nine thirty, acquire the moon. Ten, dentist” supplies scale through adjacency.
- **“Everyone works for me! It’s in the org chart!”** is the best villain line across all three. His evidence is a diagram he made.

### What’s flat
- `notes FINAL actually` and 4,812 unread: stock desktop-chaos dressing. Keep two, not twelve.
- **“Everything has a place.”** Useful setup, not a joke.
- **“For the first time in my life, I’m on top.”** Announces the turn before the picture earns it.
- **“And your place is beneath me!”** Generic trailer villain. Less funny than the org chart.
- The manic laugh spends valuable seconds saying nothing new.

### Product, beats 2–3
Best demo foundation. But the first switch appears to understand and categorize every app magically. Establish that these routing rules already exist, then demonstrate adding one.

**“It can’t interrupt me any more” overclaims.** Say it has its own workspace. Hold the bar long enough to read app icons and miniature layouts—not just admire the sliding marker. Beat 3 demonstrates outcomes, not much additional competence with the tool.

### Ending, timing, reading
Mom interrupts villainy is ancient; hiding the lair with a workspace switch is product-relevant but predictable. The whispered return adds a second ending without a second surprise. The card is better than the reveal.

At 102 seconds, cut five seconds of mess and half the shouted roll call. Fast readers discover the moon plan ahead of narration—good dramatic irony—but repeated calendar reading delays everyone else. Listeners miss the best tiny-screen details.

## B — “The rules”

### What works
- **“See? Gone. Not gone. On two.”** Excellent honest explanation in character.
- **“Mom floats. She doesn’t like being tiled.”** Funny sentence, problematic feature demonstration.
- Rejecting `beneath me` is a clean collision between grandiosity and software.
- “Fine! Everyone goes to Z!” is a satisfying petty concession.

### What’s flat
- **“People say it’s a cry for help.”** Familiar and nonspecific.
- **“I’ve organized things that don’t need it.”** Explains the premise instead of showing an example.
- **“Dave from accounting → ignore.”** Office-mug humor, and not an app rule.
- Neighbourhood → city → ocean increases geographical size, not comic invention.

### Product, beats 2–3
The clearest rule demonstration, followed immediately by muddled semantics. “Mom” suggests caller-specific routing; floating a FaceTime app would not mean floating one particular caller. Abstract concepts need to be visibly invented apps, or clearly fictional escalation after the honest demo.

Don’t present “TOML-like” configuration or exact validator messages as genuine product UI unless they are genuine. Nothing demonstrates first-match ordering. Beat 3 needs an actual switch to the routed destination.

### Ending, timing, reading
The moon rejection is clean, but the earlier validator has already taught us the ending: software refuses metaphor. It’s **the same reversal twice**. The fabricated system sheet also risks making Spaceman look patronizing.

This is the worst runtime fit. Dense rows, narration, errors and workspace montages compete for the same seconds. Fast readers finish each joke before he reacts; listeners lose the confessional accumulation that makes the draft distinctive.

## C — “You pressed it”

### What works
- Participation becoming employment is the strongest engine.
- **“I made you a workspace.”** Nicely possessive.
- **“There’s a window.”** Properly pathetic recruitment incentive.
- Capturing the surrounding page changes the apparent boundary of the animation. That is an ending-sized event.

### What’s flat
- **“My hands are… full of windows.”** Strained; the cursor already functions as his hand.
- **“We make a good team.” / “I’ve never had a team.”** Sentimental connective tissue.
- **“Do you know what that makes you? A HENCHMAN!”** We read that answer thirty seconds earlier.
- **“Not you.”** Explains the visual joke after we understood it.

### Product, beats 2–3
Beat 2 inherits A’s strengths and ambiguities. Beat 3 is barely a demo: two lines about companionship aren’t competence. Show a completed task using a workspace switch and rule.

Keep real interaction available during calm sections, but never require compliance to advance. Don’t accuse someone of pressing every key when they didn’t. Phone users need equally truthful dialogue.

### Ending, timing, reading
Strongest **visual** surprise, weakened by announcing ownership so early. Save the visitor label until the capture. Make the page theft unmistakably cartoon fiction, not implied browser-control functionality.

This has the opposite timing problem from B: too little material, inflated with response waits and shouting. Fast readers get the henchman reveal early; listeners understand the relationship but need narration to establish what page furniture is being stolen.
