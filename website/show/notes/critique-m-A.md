## 2. Does the reveal land?

**Not yet. The interview framing is under-established; the ominousness is over-signalled.**

A first-time viewer will probably understand this much:

- He has withdrawn from people into increasingly meticulous administration.
- He became obsessed with his neighbour’s hedge.
- He went over there late at night.
- The police subsequently acquired his photographs and recording.
- Something bad probably happened to the neighbour.

They will **not reliably understand that he killed and buried the neighbour**, if that is the intended conclusion. Nor will they necessarily understand why this particular account was recorded until the evidence labels supply the answer.

The problem with the “six feet” double meaning is mechanical: **“six feet” does not mean “six feet under” without some help.** “It’s six feet now. All of it” literally describes a uniformly trimmed hedge. Other viewers may imagine something involving the hedge itself. You have generated several sinister possibilities, not one devastating recognition.

“All of it” also sounds composed for an audience. It is a horror-film button delivered by a man who otherwise ought not to know he is in a horror film.

The strongest tonal line is:

> “There’s no rule about lights. I checked.”

That is the film’s governing intelligence: he has investigated whether his irritation possesses regulatory standing. It is funny without requesting laughter, and it exposes him without diagnosing him.

The weakest structural choice is putting the crucial explanation in the draft heading: **“a custody interview.”** The audience does not get that heading. “You have those” suggests an investigation, but could also belong to a deposition, insurance inquiry or neighbourhood dispute. “Exhibit 12-C” establishes evidence, not necessarily criminal custody.

My recommendation: **make the official recording context unmistakable in the final image, supply one restrained visual indication that No. 14 subsequently disappeared, and stop asking the six-feet phrase to carry the entire crime.** You can preserve uncertainty about what he did without leaving uncertainty about whether anything happened.

## 3. The five weakest lines

### 1. “The internet went to three. It isn’t closed. People always think it’s closed. It’s on two.”

**Problem:** The browser has just gone to three, then “it” is on two. This creates confusion at precisely the moment the viewer should understand the product. “People always think” also sounds like customer-support copy smuggled into testimony.

**Rewrite:**

> “The browser went to three. Option–three took me there. Still open. Option–two brought me back to Mail.”

This supplies the missing switching shortcut and makes the demonstration verifiable.

### 2. “Every morning, six-oh-two. You have those.”

**Problem:** “Six-oh-two” sounds like a time, especially in a monologue about dates, surveillance and midnight. It is not how this man would naturally report a hedge measuring six feet two inches.

**Rewrite:**

> “Six foot two. Every morning. The photographs are dated.”

His confidence in his documentation is more unsettling than a conspicuous clue that the authorities possess it.

### 3. “The twentieth. Where was I on the twentieth. I was on five.”

**Problem:** This is the writer’s cleverest line rather than the man’s most believable answer. A workspace is not a physical alibi, and he sounds momentarily aware of the film’s conceptual conceit.

**Rewrite:**

> “The twentieth? At home. I had the camera open on five.”

You retain the conflation of his life with his desktop without making him deliver a riddle.

### 4. “I’ve told you what happened. I was very clear.”

**Problem:** You have conspicuously *not* told us what happened. This is a trailer line advertising an omission. It also substitutes generic menace for his otherwise specific accounting.

**Rewrite:**

> “I came back just after midnight. That should be on the camera as well.”

Now he volunteers corroboration. That is colder, more useful to the final picture, and consistent with someone slightly pleased by the completeness of his account.

### 5. “It’s six feet now. / All of it.”

**Problem:** A punchline pretending not to be a punchline. Its literal meaning is clearer than its intended second meaning.

**Rewrite:**

> “It’s within the limit now.  
> [pause]  
> I’d like a copy of my statement.”

This shifts the ending from a macabre verbal flourish to his continuing investment in procedure. **It only works if the picture supplies the missing consequence.** No substitute closing sentence can honestly manufacture that consequence by itself.

## 4. Rule breaks and pressure points

### Product

- **Option+number is never explained.** We see switching, but the visitor is then expected to perform an unexplained operation. Fix this in speech or with an exceptionally clear prompt.
- **The “three / two” pronoun error needs fixing.** It makes correctly functioning workspaces sound inconsistent.
- **“I wrote a rule for Dana” suggests person-based routing.** The next sentence partially corrects it, but the product routes an app or window title—not a correspondent as such. Prefer:
  > “I set Chatter to open on two. The whole window went. Her message was still in it.”
- The bottom bar must visibly show **both numbered workspaces and their contents**. The spoken description does not quite establish the latter.
- “Four was the house. Five was the street” is fine as his description of his arrangement. **Do not turn those descriptions into workspace names in the interface.**
- The evidence-review desktop should also avoid overlapping windows. Do not accidentally finish your tiling-manager film with a beautiful floating-window composition.

### Performance and framing

- **“That wasn’t — it’s just where it goes” is manufactured evasiveness.** Nobody has audibly accused him of hiding Dana. Let her unanswered message do that work.
- “I was on five” and “All of it” are the nearest things to winks. Both offer the audience a satisfying phrase at the expense of the speaker’s credibility.
- The six-second interaction needs a **deliberate recording pause**, supported by interface text. He cannot suddenly invite website visitors to try the shortcut without breaking the retrospective interview.
- The script is roughly **230 words**. At an unhurried 120 words per minute, that is about 115 seconds before the visitor interval or substantial visual holds. It fits the upper spoken-duration limit, but not comfortably inside this particular 100-second film. Cut toward **180–200 words**, rather than accelerating the performance.

### Harm and real-world references

No direct breach. The fictional dispute, implied crime and reported speech are within the brief; nothing here is an instruction for harm. Keep the violence unshown and the other caller unheard.

**Not merely an evidence label. A recognisable piece of his ordinary desktop, now being used to examine his account.**

After the pull-back through the dark house, cut to one restrained, tiled review desktop:

- **Another pane:** the audio playing under an unambiguous, mundane heading such as **“Police recorded interview — 02.”** The voice continues without interruption. This is the instant the recording changes category.

If you choose this, that glimpse must actually exist earlier; do not label an illuminated window a sighting.

No blood. No grave diagram. No “HOMICIDE” banner. No red string. No tiny paragraph the viewer must pause to decipher.
